# DX601 Week 6 — Designing Statistical Experiments

## Big Picture

Week 6 is about turning vague real-world questions into scientifically testable experiments.

Main workflow:

Ambiguous Question → Research Questions → Hypotheses → Experiment Design → Statistical Conclusions

The major ideas are:
- Break broad questions into precise research questions.
- Convert those questions into testable hypotheses.
- Control unwanted sources of variability.
- Choose meaningful baselines/controls.
- Make experiments reproducible and, when possible, replicable.
- Identify threats to validity.
- Balance experimental rigor against practical constraints.

## 1. From Ambiguous Questions to Hypotheses

High-level questions are usually too vague to test directly.

Examples:
- Which model is better?
- Is this increase in traffic meaningful?
- Are users buying two products together on purpose or by chance?

A statistical hypothesis is usually closer to a binary question:

> Does XX happen or not?

So the first step is to identify the ambiguity hidden inside the original question.

## 2. Example: “Which Model Is Better?”

This question is incomplete because several details are missing.

### Dataset

A model may perform better on one dataset but not another.

So ask:

> Better on what dataset or in what context?

### Performance Metric

Different metrics answer different questions.

Examples:
- accuracy
- false positive rate
- precision
- recall
- another task-specific metric

So ask:

> Better according to what measurement?

### Model Initialization

Some models depend on random initialization.

A specific trained model performing well does not automatically mean that the whole model type is better.

You may need to distinguish:
- Is this specific trained model better?
- Is this type of model generally better after repeated training?

## 3. Generating Research Questions

The lesson's procedure is:

1. Start with the broad question.
2. Find every important ambiguity.
3. List possible ways each ambiguity can be resolved.
4. Consider combinations of those resolutions.
5. Turn each combination into a precise research question.
6. Convert those research questions into testable hypotheses.

Conceptually:

Broad Question → Resolve Ambiguities → Unambiguous Research Questions → Hypotheses

A single business question can therefore produce multiple hypotheses.

## 4. Hypotheses Should Be Specific

Instead of:

> Are these products being bought together more often?

you may need to investigate:
- Is the increase statistically significant relative to past purchasing behavior?
- Are similar products also increasing?
- Is the increase seasonal?
- Is there a coupon or discount causing the change?
- Is the increase concentrated in certain locations, times, or user groups?

The goal is to separate possible explanations rather than treating everything as one question.

## 5. Designing a Single Experiment

Once you have a hypothesis, design an experiment that isolates the factor you care about.

A well-designed experiment tries to minimize extra variability.

Simple idea:

> Vary as close to one thing at a time as possible.

Hidden variability can make it impossible to know what actually caused the observed difference.

## 6. Important Sources of Variability in Machine Learning

The lesson emphasizes two especially common hidden sources.

### Random Initialization

Many models are trained using randomized procedures.

Different initial parameter values can lead to different final trained models.

Therefore:

> One good or bad training run does not necessarily represent the model type as a whole.

You may need multiple runs to estimate typical performance.

### Number of Parameters / Model Capacity

Models with different numbers of parameters may have different abilities to learn the task.

If one model has much more capacity than another, comparing them directly may not be apples-to-apples.

Important distinction:

> Equal number of parameters does not always mean equal capacity.

Different architectures can use parameters differently.

The real goal is to control model capacity, not blindly the raw parameter count.

## 7. Controls

A control is the baseline you compare against.

If an experiment measures the effect of a manipulation, the control is usually the unmanipulated or reference condition.

Examples:
- Drug experiment → group that does not receive the drug
- Model comparison → baseline model
- Statistical comparison → assumption of no difference

For two models, a common null assumption is:

\[
\text{mean performance difference} = 0
\]

## 8. Choosing a Good Baseline

A baseline should be meaningful.

A model can be technically “simple” but still be a bad baseline.

The lesson gives an example involving a majority-class classifier and hold-one-out cross validation. In a perfectly balanced dataset, removing one test point makes the remaining training set favor the opposite class, causing this baseline to systematically predict the test point incorrectly.

Main lesson:

> A baseline that is trivially easy to beat tells you very little.

You must justify why the baseline makes sense for the actual task.

## 9. Reproducibility vs. Replicability

### Reproducibility

Reproducibility means rerunning the same experiment/code produces the same result.

In computing, this often requires controlling randomness.

A common technique is setting random seeds.

Examples include seeds for:
- model initialization
- data splitting
- random number generators
- language/runtime-specific randomness

### Replicability

Replicability means reaching the same conclusion through a fresh experiment, potentially using:
- different data
- different methods
- another implementation

So:
- Reproducibility = same setup, same result.
- Replicability = new experiment, same general conclusion.

Repeated experiments and sufficient sample sizes help reduce the influence of random chance.

## 10. Blocking

Blocking means grouping similar experimental subjects together to control unwanted variability.

Examples in machine learning:
- tree-based models
- Bayesian models
- neural models
- models grouped by size/capacity

Why block?

Because some hidden difference between categories may otherwise affect your results.

Blocking attempts to make comparisons within more comparable groups.

## 11. Common Experimental Flaws

The lesson highlights several major problems.

### Overfitting / Memorization

A model can appear to perform well because it memorized the available data rather than learning something that generalizes.

### Small Sample Sizes

Too little data makes conclusions highly sensitive to random chance.

Larger samples generally improve the reliability of statistical conclusions.

### Dataset Problems

When using someone else's dataset, you inherit its flaws.

Possible issues:
- dataset does not represent deployment conditions
- sampling bias
- idealized examples
- missing difficult cases
- unrealistic environments

Always examine the dataset documentation and ask whether the data matches the real use case.

## 12. Challenge Datasets

A challenge dataset deliberately includes difficult or realistic cases.

The lesson uses street-sign recognition as an example.

Training data may contain:
- centered signs
- good lighting
- clear visibility

But real deployment may involve:
- poor lighting
- snow
- occlusion
- unusual placement
- partial visibility

So good experimental design should include difficult real-world conditions when those conditions matter to deployment.

## 13. Synthetic / Fake Samples

Synthetic samples can be created by modifying real samples.

Examples:
- adding noise
- adding occlusion
- changing lighting
- other artificial distortions

These can help a model learn robustness.

However:

> Synthetic data is not automatically equivalent to real-world difficult data.

Your synthetic generation process may miss important real-world conditions.

So synthetic samples should not necessarily replace real challenge datasets.

## 14. Match the Experiment to Deployment

A central lesson is:

> Your data and simulation environment should be as faithful to the real deployment setting as possible.

Performance under ideal laboratory conditions does not guarantee performance in real use.

## 15. “Lemming Behavior”

The lesson uses lemming behavior to describe blindly copying previous researchers or competitors because “that's what everyone else does.”

Examples:
- copying a competitor's hyperparameters
- using powers of 2 just because they are common
- copying published performance numbers
- copying someone else's experimental configuration

The danger is that you also inherit their mistakes.

Before using someone else's setup, ask:
- Was their configuration scientifically justified?
- Was their data/setup the same as yours?
- Did they measure the metric you actually care about?
- Can you reproduce their result yourself?

## 16. Comparing Against Published Results

Be careful when directly comparing your model against someone else's reported score.

Potential problems:
1. Their score may be incorrect or unreliable.
2. Their experiment may use a different setting.
3. Their metric may not match your goal.
4. Their dataset or preprocessing may differ.
5. Their configuration may contain hidden assumptions.

A stronger comparison is often to:
- reproduce their work, or
- run their implementation in your own experimental setting.

## 17. Threats to Validity

A threat to validity is anything about your experimental design that could cause another experimenter to get a different or non-comparable result.

Examples:
- number of cross-validation folds
- hardware
- operating system
- software/library versions
- random seeds
- dataset selection
- preprocessing choices
- hyperparameters
- model size
- initialization
- evaluation metrics

Useful question:

> What decisions did I make when designing this experiment?

If someone could make a different reasonable decision and get a different result, that decision may belong in the threats-to-validity section.

## 18. Feasibility vs. Rigor

Ideal experiments can be expensive.

Costs may include:
- compute time
- money
- electricity
- engineering effort
- data collection
- repeated model training

This creates a tradeoff:

Scientific Rigor ↔ Practical Feasibility

For example, cross-validation may be scientifically desirable but extremely expensive if every training run takes days or weeks.

Main lesson:

> If you reduce rigor because of cost, you must be careful not to draw conclusions that the experiment cannot statistically support.

At minimum, the experiment should still provide enough evidence for statistical significance.

## 19. No Universal Minimum Experimental Size

There is not always a universal answer for how many:
- folds
- trials
- samples
- repeated experiments

are enough.

The appropriate minimum depends on the context and statistical method.

The lesson notes that researchers disagree about minimum cross-validation fold counts for some analyses.

Therefore, experimental design may require reading domain-specific academic literature.

## 20. Composing Multiple Experiments

A complicated question may require several experiments.

Each experiment should investigate one precise question.

The lesson compares this to detective work:

> Each experiment is one clue.

A single experiment contains uncertainty, but several related experiments can overlap and provide a stronger overall picture.

This does not mean all experiments or samples should automatically be treated as equivalent.

## Practical Experiment-Design Checklist

1. What is the exact research question?
2. What ambiguities remain?
3. What is the hypothesis?
4. What is the null hypothesis?
5. What variable am I trying to measure?
6. What other variables could affect the result?
7. What should be controlled?
8. What baseline/control should I use?
9. Is the baseline meaningful?
10. Do random initialization or model capacity matter?
11. Is my dataset representative of deployment?
12. Do I need challenge data?
13. Do I need multiple runs?
14. Is the experiment reproducible?
15. Could the conclusion replicate with fresh data?
16. What are the threats to validity?
17. Is the sample size large enough?
18. Am I sacrificing rigor because of feasibility?
19. What conclusions does the evidence actually support?

## Quick Memory

- Broad questions usually become multiple research questions and hypotheses.
- Resolve ambiguity before designing the experiment.
- A hypothesis should be precise enough to test statistically.
- Good experiments minimize extra variability.
- Random initialization can change model performance.
- Parameter count and model capacity can confound comparisons.
- A control is the baseline you compare against.
- A bad baseline can make results meaningless.
- Reproducibility = same setup produces the same result.
- Replicability = fresh experiment reaches the same conclusion.
- Blocking groups similar subjects/models to control variability.
- Inherited dataset flaws become flaws in your experiment.
- Synthetic data can help, but it may not replace real challenge data.
- Match your experiment as closely as possible to deployment conditions.
- Don't copy configurations or reported scores blindly.
- Track threats to validity throughout the experiment.
- Better rigor usually means more time, money, data, or compute.
- If rigor is reduced, do not overstate the conclusion.
- Multiple focused experiments can combine into a stronger overall picture.
