# DX601 Week 5 — Markov, Chebyshev, and Chernoff Bounds

## Big Picture

Week 5 moves from calculating **exact probabilities** to calculating **probability bounds**.

A bound may be easier to calculate and still be useful enough for making guarantees.

The three major inequalities are:

1. **Markov's inequality**
2. **Chebyshev's inequality**
3. **Chernoff / Chernoff-Hoeffding bounds**

They become progressively more specialized and can give progressively tighter guarantees when their assumptions apply.

---

## 1. Exact Probability vs. Probability Bounds

A **lower bound** says a quantity is at least some value.

\[
X\ge L
\]

An **upper bound** says a quantity is at most some value.

\[
X\le U
\]

For probabilities, the trivial bounds are always:

\[
0\le Pr[E]\le1
\]

but these are usually too loose to be useful.

### Why use a bound?

Exact probability may be:

- difficult to compute
- tedious because of many cases
- impossible to compute exactly
- unnecessary when only a guarantee is needed

A useful bound can simplify the problem while still giving enough information to make a decision.

---

## 2. Complement Trick

Sometimes \(Pr[E]\) is hard to calculate directly, but \(Pr[\neg E]\) is easy.

Use:

\[
Pr[E]+Pr[\neg E]=1
\]

so:

\[
Pr[E]=1-Pr[\neg E]
\]

The lesson's sampling-above-the-median example uses this idea.

If failure occurs only when all \(k\) samples are below the median:

\[
Pr[\text{failure}]\le\left(\frac12\right)^k
\]

Therefore:

\[
Pr[\text{success}]\ge1-\left(\frac12\right)^k
\]

The failure probability decreases exponentially as \(k\) increases.

---

## 3. Tightness of a Bound

**Tightness** describes how close a bound is to the exact probability.

- A tight upper bound is close to the true value from above.
- A tight lower bound is close to the true value from below.
- A loose bound may be mathematically correct but practically unhelpful.

General rule:

> Use the most specialized bound whose assumptions apply.

More assumptions can allow a tighter guarantee.

---

# Markov's Inequality

## 4. Formula

For a **non-negative** random variable \(X\):

\[
Pr[X\ge a]\le\frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{a}
\]

where \(a>0\).

### What you need

- \(X\ge0\)
- \(\mathbb{E}[X]\)

You do **not** need to know the full distribution.

---

## 5. Intuition for Markov

If the average value of \(X\) is small, then \(X\) cannot take very large values very often.

Example idea:

If:

\[
\mathbb{E}[X]=10
\]

then:

\[
Pr[X\ge100]\le\frac{10}{100}=0.1
\]

So the probability that \(X\) is at least 100 is at most 10%.

### Strength and weakness

**Strength:** Very general.

**Weakness:** Often loose because it only uses the expected value.

---

# Chebyshev's Inequality

## 6. Formula

For a random variable \(X\) with expected value and variance:

\[
Pr\left[|X-\mathbb{E}[X]|\ge b\right]
\le
\frac{\mathbb{V}[X]}{b^2}
\]

Unlike Markov, \(X\) may take negative values.

### What you need

- \(\mathbb{E}[X]\)
- \(\mathbb{V}[X]\)

Chebyshev measures the probability that \(X\) is at least distance \(b\) away from its mean.

---

## 7. Why Chebyshev Comes from Markov

Start with:

\[
|X-\mathbb{E}[X]|\ge b
\]

Square both sides:

\[
(X-\mathbb{E}[X])^2\ge b^2
\]

Let:

\[
Z=(X-\mathbb{E}[X])^2
\]

Since \(Z\ge0\), apply Markov:

\[
Pr[Z\ge b^2]\le\frac{\mathbb{E}[Z]}{b^2}
\]

But:

\[
\mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}[X])^2]=\mathbb{V}[X]
\]

Therefore:

\[
Pr\left[|X-\mathbb{E}[X]|\ge b\right]
\le
\frac{\mathbb{V}[X]}{b^2}
\]

---

## 8. Important Chebyshev Warning

Chebyshev bounds the **combined deviation in both directions**.

Without extra assumptions such as symmetry, you cannot simply divide the bound in half and assign half to each tail.

---

## 9. Coin-Flip Example

Suppose:

\[
Pr[Heads]=p
\]

Flip the coin \(n\) times and define:

\[
\bar{X}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i
\]

The lesson derives:

\[
\mathbb{E}[\bar{X}]=p
\]

and:

\[
\mathbb{V}[\bar{X}]=\frac{p(1-p)}{n}
\]

Applying Chebyshev to the event:

\[
\left|\bar{X}-p\right|\ge\frac{p}{c}
\]

gives:

\[
Pr\left[|\bar{X}-p|\ge\frac{p}{c}\right]
\le
\frac{c^2(1-p)}{pn}
\]

So the deviation bound decreases like:

\[
O\left(\frac1n\right)
\]

### Main takeaway

More samples make it less likely that the empirical average is far from the true probability \(p\).

---

# Chernoff's Inequality

## 10. General Chernoff Bound

For \(t>0\):

\[
Pr[X\ge a]
\le
\frac{\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]}{e^{ta}}
\]

The parameter \(t\) can be chosen to tighten the bound:

\[
Pr[X\ge a]
\le
\min_{t>0}
\frac{\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]}{e^{ta}}
\]

This tunability is one major advantage over Markov and Chebyshev.

---

# Chernoff-Hoeffding Bound

## 11. Formula

Suppose:

\[
X=\sum_{i=1}^n X_i
\]

where the \(X_i\) are independent and:

\[
X_i\in[a_i,b_i]
\]

Then:

\[
Pr\left[|X-\mathbb{E}[X]|\ge a\right]
\le
2\exp\left(
-\frac{2a^2}
{\sum_{i=1}^n(b_i-a_i)^2}
\right)
\]

### What you need

- \(X\) is a sum of independent random variables.
- Each \(X_i\) has a bounded range \([a_i,b_i]\).

This is more specialized than Markov or Chebyshev.

---

## 12. Why Chernoff/Hoeffding Is Powerful

For many problems, the bound decreases **exponentially** with sample size.

That is much faster than the \(O(1/n)\) decrease obtained in the Chebyshev coin-flip example.

---

## 13. Coin-Flip Hoeffding Example

For Bernoulli coin flips:

\[
X_i\in[0,1]
\]

so:

\[
(b_i-a_i)^2=1
\]

and:

\[
\sum_{i=1}^n(b_i-a_i)^2=n
\]

For a fixed deviation \(c\), the lesson obtains:

\[
Pr[|\bar{X}-p|>c]
\le
2\exp(-2c^2n)
\]

### Main takeaway

The chance that the observed average differs from the true probability by more than \(c\) decreases exponentially as \(n\) increases.

---

## 14. Bounds and Confidence Intervals

The Chernoff/Hoeffding result can be written as:

\[
Pr[\bar{X}\notin[p-c,p+c]]
\le
2\exp(-2c^2n)
\]

For confidence level \(\alpha\), the lesson relates this to:

\[
2\exp(-2c^2n)\le1-\alpha
\]

You can solve this inequality for \(n\) to determine how many samples are needed for a desired guarantee.

---

# 15. Markov vs. Chebyshev vs. Chernoff/Hoeffding

| Bound | Main Requirements | What It Bounds | Typical Quality |
|---|---|---|---|
| Markov | \(X\ge0\), know \(\mathbb{E}[X]\) | Large values of \(X\) | Most general, often loosest |
| Chebyshev | Know \(\mathbb{E}[X]\) and \(\mathbb{V}[X]\) | Deviation from the mean | Tighter than Markov in many cases |
| Chernoff / Hoeffding | Independent bounded random variables / exponential-moment setup | Tail/deviation probabilities | More specialized, often much tighter |

The key pattern is:

\[
\text{More assumptions}
\Rightarrow
\text{more specialized bound}
\Rightarrow
\text{potentially tighter guarantee}
\]

---
